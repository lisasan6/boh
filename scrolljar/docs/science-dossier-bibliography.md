# ScrollJar Science Dossier — Annotated Bibliography
*Peer-reviewed evidence on social media's addictive design and what actually reduces use. Compiled July 2026. All citations verified against journal pages.*

## 1. Causal economics: social media harms welfare, and users know it

**Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow (2020), "The Welfare Effects of Social Media," *American Economic Review* 110(3): 629–76.** RCT paying users to deactivate Facebook for 4 weeks before the 2018 midterms: deactivation increased subjective well-being, increased offline socializing, reduced polarization, and caused a large *persistent* drop in post-experiment Facebook use and valuation. Why it matters: the flagship RCT showing that stepping away improves well-being — and that a forced break breaks the habit, exactly ScrollJar's mechanism. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190658

**Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022), "Digital Addiction," *American Economic Review* 112(7): 2424–63.** Field experiment with screen-time tools: self-control problems cause an estimated **31% of social media use**; letting people set binding future limits substantially reduces use; temporary incentives have persistent effects; users are partially unaware of their own habit formation — and are willing to pay for commitment technology. Why it matters: ScrollJar's core premise (people rationally pay to lock themselves out) is the headline result of an AER paper. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20210867

**Bursztyn, Handel, Jiménez-Durán & Roth (2025), "When Product Markets Become Collective Traps: The Case of Social Media," *American Economic Review* 115(12): 4105–36 (NBER WP 31771, 2023).** Incentivized experiments with college students show many *active* users would prefer TikTok/Instagram not exist at all; accounting for the externality on non-users flips measured welfare from positive to negative — a "collective trap" driven by fear of missing out. Why it matters: users aren't choosing freely; they're trapped. ScrollJar is an individual exit from a collective trap. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20231468 · https://home.uchicago.edu/bursztyn/CollectiveTraps.pdf

**Braghieri, Levy & Makarin (2022), "Social Media and Mental Health," *American Economic Review* 112(11): 3660–93.** Quasi-experiment using Facebook's staggered 2004–06 college rollout: access worsened student mental health — severe depression up ~7%, anxiety disorder up ~20%, academic impairment up 3pp on a 13% base — via unfavorable social comparison, with increased psychotherapy/antidepressant uptake among the most vulnerable. Why it matters: the strongest causal evidence that social media itself (not screens generally) damages mental health. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20211218 · https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/social-media-and-mental-health

## 2. Addictive design: the slot machine in your pocket

**Schultz, Dayan & Montague (1997), "A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward," *Science* 275: 1593–99.** Dopamine neurons fire on reward *prediction error* — unpredictable rewards drive learning far more than expected ones. Why it matters: the neuroscientific basis for why variable-reward feeds (pull-to-refresh, unpredictable likes) are so compulsive. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593

**Schüll (2012), *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas*, Princeton University Press.** Ethnography of how slot machines are engineered for "time on device" and the dissociative "machine zone." Why it matters: the canonical scholarly account of intentionally addictive design; tech insiders explicitly borrowed these patterns. https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691160887/addiction-by-design

**Sherman, Payton, Hernandez, Greenfield & Dapretto (2016), "The Power of the Like in Adolescence," *Psychological Science* 27(7): 1027–35.** fMRI: viewing one's photos with many likes activates the nucleus accumbens (reward circuitry), especially in adolescents, and peers' likes shift behavior. Why it matters: direct neural evidence that social feedback in apps engages reward machinery. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616645673

**Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita (2012), "Habits Make Smartphone Use More Pervasive," *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 16: 105–14.** Smartphone use is dominated by brief, automatic "checking habits" cued by context and reinforced by quick informational rewards. Why it matters: scrolling is habit, not decision — so interventions must interrupt the cue-action loop, not rely on willpower. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2

## 3. Friction works

**Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023), "Directing Smartphone Use Through the Self-Nudge App one sec," *PNAS* 120(8): e2213114120.** 6-week field study (n=280) + preregistered online experiment (n≈500): a brief friction screen led users to abort **36% of app-opening attempts** in the moment; opening *attempts* fell ~37% over six weeks (combined ~57% fewer openings); the "dismiss" option was the most effective ingredient, a deliberation message alone did nothing. Why it matters: friction demonstrably works and compounds over time — ScrollJar's blocking layer stands on PNAS-level evidence; its financial stake adds what friction lacks (an override cost). https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2213114120

**Castelo, Kushlev, Ward, Esterman & Reiner (2025), "Blocking Mobile Internet on Smartphones Improves Sustained Attention, Mental Health, and Subjective Well-Being," *PNAS Nexus* 4(2): pgaf017.** RCT (~400+ participants) blocking mobile internet for two weeks improved objectively measured sustained attention, mental health, and well-being — with a large share of participants improving on at least one outcome — mediated by more time offline, socializing, and sleeping. Why it matters: hard blocking (not just tracking) causally improves attention and mood; ScrollJar's block-first design is the evidence-backed one. https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf017/8016017

## 4. Commitment devices and financial stakes

**Giné, Karlan & Zinman (2010), "Put Your Money Where Your Butt Is: A Commitment Contract for Smoking Cessation," *AEJ: Applied Economics* 2(4): 213–35.** RCT in the Philippines: smokers offered a voluntary deposit contract (money forfeited if a urine test showed nicotine) were significantly more likely to pass at 6 and 12 months (ITT ~3–6pp, ~30–60% over baseline quit rates). Why it matters: the founding field evidence that self-imposed financial forfeits change hard, addictive behavior. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.2.4.213

**Halpern et al. (2015), "Randomized Trial of Four Financial-Incentive Programs for Smoking Cessation," *NEJM* 372: 2108–17.** N=2,538 CVS employees: deposit-based programs had lower uptake than pure rewards, but among willing participants deposits were substantially *more effective* than rewards (~13.2pp higher sustained abstinence in instrumental-variable analysis). Why it matters: loss of one's own money beats equivalent rewards — the behavioral engine of ScrollJar's penalty. Design lesson: keep stakes self-set and low-friction to solve the uptake problem. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293

**de Buisonjé et al. (2022), "Investigating Rewards and Deposit Contract Financial Incentives for Physical Activity Behavior Change Using a Smartphone App: RCT," *JMIR* 24(10): e38339.** App-administered deposit contracts increased goal achievement for physical activity, showing deposit mechanics port cleanly into smartphone apps (see also Stedman-Falls & Dallery 2020, *JABA* 53(4), showing tech-based deposit contracts work as well as in-person). Why it matters: direct precedent for app-based deposit contracts; note **no peer-reviewed evaluation of Forfeit, Opal, or any stakes-based screen-time app yet exists** — a first-mover research opportunity for ScrollJar. https://www.jmir.org/2022/10/e38339 · https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jaba.776

## 5. Penalty framing: charity, fines, and shame

**Gneezy & Rustichini (2000), "A Fine Is a Price," *Journal of Legal Studies* 29(1): 1–17.** Introducing a small fine for late daycare pickup *increased* lateness — a fine can be re-read as a fair price that licenses the behavior. Why it matters: ScrollJar's key design caution — penalties must be self-set and large enough to sting, framed as broken commitments, not as a purchase of scroll time. https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/468061

**Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg (2015), "A Meta-Analytic Review of Moral Licensing," *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 41(4): 540–58.** Moral licensing is real but small (d≈0.31) and context-dependent. Why it matters: the "charity framing weakens deterrence" worry is legitimate but modest; the loss-aversion effect of forfeiting money is far larger, and charity destination avoids the perverse incentive of the app profiting from failure. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167215572134

**Breines & Chen (2012), "Self-Compassion Increases Self-Improvement Motivation," *PSPB* 38(9): 1133–43.** Self-compassion after failure increases motivation to improve more than self-criticism does. Why it matters: post-override messaging should be compassionate, not shaming — shame predicts relapse, compassion predicts retry. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167212445599

**Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38: 69–119.** Meta-analysis of 94 studies: if-then plans improve goal attainment with medium-to-large effect (d=0.65). Why it matters: onboarding should elicit concrete if-then plans ("if I reach for TikTok after 9pm, then I open my book"), not vague goals. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1

## 6. Newest reduction RCTs and the detox record

**Schmidt-Persson et al. (2024), "Screen Media Use and Mental Health of Children and Adolescents," *JAMA Network Open* 7(7): e2419881.** Danish cluster RCT: families limiting leisure screen use to ≤3 hrs/week for two weeks saw significant improvement in children's behavioral difficulties, especially internalizing symptoms and prosocial behavior. https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2821176

**Thai et al. (2023), "Reducing Social Media Use Improves Appearance and Weight Esteem in Youth with Emotional Distress," *Psychology of Popular Media*.** RCT: capping social media at 1 hr/day for 3 weeks improved body image in distressed teens/young adults. https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000460

**Brailovskaia et al. (2023), "Finding the 'Sweet Spot' of Smartphone Use," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 29(1).** Cutting use by 1 hr/day for one week produced lower use, higher life satisfaction and physical activity, sustained at 4-month follow-up — reduction beat total abstinence. https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000430

**Radtke et al. (2022), "Digital Detox: An Effective Solution in the Smartphone Era? A Systematic Literature Review," *Mobile Media & Communication* 10(2).** Cold-turkey detox findings are mixed and effects often fade. Why it matters (with Brailovskaia): sustainable *reduction with structural support* beats abstinence — ScrollJar's model. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20501579211028647

## 7. The adolescent-harm debate, stated fairly

**Orben & Przybylski (2019), *Nature Human Behaviour* 3: 173–82** (specification-curve analysis of 350k+ adolescents: digital tech use explains ≤0.4% of well-being variance — comparable to eating potatoes) vs. **Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024)** and Twenge, who argue phones caused the post-2012 teen mental-health decline; **Odgers (2024), *Nature*** reviews the causal case as unproven; Haidt's team's 2026 rebuttal continues the exchange. Honest synthesis for ScrollJar: *average correlational* effects are small, but the best *causal* designs (Braghieri et al.; deactivation RCTs; Castelo et al.) find real harms concentrated in heavy users and vulnerable groups — precisely the self-selected users who reach for a commitment device. ScrollJar needn't overclaim population-level catastrophe; the individual-level causal evidence is sufficient and stronger. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1 · https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2
