# ScrollJar Science Dossier — Annotated Bibliography
*Peer-reviewed evidence on habit-forming design and what actually helps people use their phones less. First compiled July 2026. **Re-verified and corrected 15 October 2026:** every entry was checked against the paper itself (abstract or full text), plus Crossref and OpenAlex for retractions and corrections, and searched for failed replications. The full audit, with quotes and links, is in `science-verification-2026-10/`, and the summary is in `science-verified-2026-10.md`.*

**How to read this file.** Each entry says what the study actually found, how strong it is (design and sample size), and what we may honestly say. ⚠️ marks a limitation that matters for public claims. If a claim isn't in this file or in `psychology-playbook.md`, don't make it.

**The honest headline (use this framing everywhere):** cutting back on social media and phone use produces **small but real** benefits in randomised trials, and these come from **sustained reduction**, not short detoxes. Friction and self-set limits measurably reduce use. Money stakes work for the minority who choose them. None of this is a cure, and we never imply clinical effects.

## 1. Causal evidence: what stepping back does

**Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow (2020), "The Welfare Effects of Social Media," *American Economic Review* 110(3): 629–76.** doi:10.1257/aer.20190658. RCT: adults paid to deactivate Facebook for the 4 weeks before the 2018 US midterms. Results:
- **"small but significant"** gains in well-being (0.09 SD);
- more time offline with friends and family;
- less news knowledge;
- Facebook use about 22% lower for weeks afterwards.

⚠️ The polarisation result did *not* hold in the much larger 2020 follow-up (below), so don't cite it. **Say:** "In a large randomised trial, four weeks off Facebook brought small but significant gains in well-being and more time with friends and family, and people kept using it about a fifth less for weeks afterwards."

**Allcott, Gentzkow, Wittenbrink et al. (2025), "The Effect of Deactivating Facebook and Instagram on Users' Emotional State," NBER Working Paper 33697.** doi:10.3386/w33697. This is the largest trial so far (about 35,000 adults, six weeks before the 2020 US election). Deactivating Facebook improved an index of happiness, depression and anxiety by **0.06 SD**, driven by people over 35; the Instagram effect was smaller and not significant after the preregistered correction. The authors say effects are "smaller than benchmarks … and previous experimental estimates in smaller samples." ⚠️ A working paper, not yet peer-reviewed. The companion paper (*PNAS* 2024, 121(21): e2321584121, correction issued 2026) found effects on polarisation "close to zero." **Say:** "In the largest trial to date, six weeks off Facebook slightly improved mood (working paper)."

**Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022), "Digital Addiction," *American Economic Review* 112(7): 2424–63.** doi:10.1257/aer.20210867. Field experiment with about 2,000 US Android users:
- Their economic model estimates that "self-control problems cause **31 percent** of social media use." That is 48 minutes a day across Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, Snapchat, browsers and YouTube.
- Given a limit tool, **78% set binding limits**, and limits cut that screen time by **22 minutes a day (16%)** over 12 weeks.
- 58% would give up some money to keep the limit tool; the average was $4.20 for three weeks.

⚠️ The 31% is a model estimate, not a direct measurement. Willingness to pay was for *access to a limit tool*, not for a penalty, so don't call it "the headline result" or proof that people want to pay to lock themselves out. **Say:** "A major field experiment estimated that self-control problems account for about a third of time on social apps; most people chose to set binding limits, which cut their use by 16%."

**Bursztyn, Handel, Jiménez-Durán & Roth (2025), "When Product Markets Become Collective Traps: The Case of Social Media," *American Economic Review* 115(12): 4105–36.** doi:10.1257/aer.20231468. These were incentivised experiments with about 1,000 US college students. In the working paper, 60% of active TikTok users and 46% of active Instagram users had negative welfare from the platform existing. The most common motive was fear of missing out. ⚠️ The authors stress that such traps "can arise … even with fully rational expectations," and that leaving alone is costly, which is why people stay. So don't say "users aren't choosing freely," and don't call ScrollJar "an exit from the trap." **Say:** "Many active TikTok and Instagram users say they'd prefer the platform didn't exist for anyone, but stay so they don't miss out. ScrollJar lets you cut back without having to quit."

**Braghieri, Levy & Makarin (2022), "Social Media and Mental Health," *American Economic Review* 112(11): 3660–93.** doi:10.1257/aer.20211218. A quasi-experiment using Facebook's staggered 2004–06 rollout across US colleges.
- Students' poor-mental-health index rose by 0.085 SD, about a fifth of the effect of losing a job.
- In the authors' press materials, severe depression rose ~7% and anxiety disorders ~20%. Both are **relative to the baseline share** of students, not percentage points.
- Effects were largest for the most vulnerable students, with social comparison a likely channel.

⚠️ It covers desktop-era Facebook among students, and individual outcomes are only marginal after multiple-testing correction. It is *strong* causal evidence, not "the strongest." **Say:** "When Facebook arrived on US campuses in 2004–06, students' mental health worsened, most for the most vulnerable."

**Pieh, Humer, Hoenigl et al. (2025), "Smartphone screen time reduction improves mental health: a randomized controlled trial," *BMC Medicine* 23: 107.** doi:10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z. A preregistered RCT with 111 university students, limiting smartphone time to two hours a day for three weeks. It found "small to medium" improvements in depressive symptoms, stress, sleep quality and well-being. ⚠️ The sample is small. "Screen time increased rapidly after the intervention," so benefits need ongoing support, which is an argument for a tool rather than a one-off detox. **Say:** "Limiting phone time to two hours a day for three weeks improved sleep, stress and mood in a small trial. Use crept back once the limit ended."

**Burnell et al. (2025), "The effects of social media restriction: Meta-analytic evidence from randomized controlled trials," *SSM – Mental Health* 7: 100459.** doi:10.1016/j.ssmmh.2025.100459. Across 32 RCTs with 5,544 adults and college students, restricting social media improved subjective well-being (g = 0.17), which the authors call "small … only weak support." **Say:** "Across 32 randomised trials, limiting social media produced small but reliable well-being gains."

**Thrul et al. (2025), reanalysis in *Psychology of Popular Media* 14(2): 207–09.** doi:10.1037/ppm0000574. Interventions shorter than a week made mental health slightly *worse* (d = −0.175). Those of a week or longer improved it (d = 0.156). **Why it matters:** sustained reduction, not a weekend detox, is what helps.

## 2. Habit-forming design: why the reach is automatic
*(Retitled from "the slot machine in your pocket." Whether heavy phone use is a true "addiction" is contested among researchers, so we say "habit-forming" or "attention-capturing" design and "problematic use," never "addiction" about people.)*

**Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita (2012), "Habits Make Smartphone Use More Pervasive," *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 16(1): 105–14.** doi:10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2. Much smartphone use comes as brief, automatic "checking habits" triggered by context and reinforced by quick bits of new information. Users experienced it "more as an annoyance than an addiction." ⚠️ The study is small and from the early smartphone era, and it is about *checking*, not scrolling. **Say:** "Much phone use is brief, automatic checking triggered by context, which is why we act at the moment of the reach instead of relying on willpower."

**Wood, Quinn & Kashy (2002), "Habits in Everyday Life: Thought, Emotion, and Action," *JPSP* 83(6): 1281–97.** doi:10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281. In diary studies with students, about 43% of everyday behaviours were performed almost daily in the same context, typically while thinking about something else. ⚠️ Students; diary method.

**Lindström et al. (2021), "A computational reward learning account of social media engagement," *Nature Communications* 12: 1311.** doi:10.1038/s41467-020-19607-x. An analysis of over a million posts from 4,000+ people found that "human behavior on social media conforms … to the principles of reward learning." An online experiment (n = 176) showed social rewards causally shape posting. **Why it matters:** this is human, real-world evidence for the reward-learning account, so use it in place of animal dopamine studies.

**Schultz, Dayan & Montague (1997), "A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward," *Science* 275(5306): 1593–99.** doi:10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. In monkeys, dopamine neurons signal reward *prediction error*: rewards better than expected drive learning. ⚠️ The study is about monkeys and conditioning, not apps. Many researchers see it as one reason unpredictable rewards form strong habits, but nobody has shown at the dopamine level that "variable-reward feeds are compulsive." Background only. Never say "dopamine" in marketing.

**Schüll (2012), *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas*, Princeton University Press.** An ethnography of how slot machines are engineered to maximise "time on device" and draw players into the trance-like "machine zone." ⚠️ The book is about casinos. It is *not* evidence that tech companies copied these patterns, so that claim needs its own source or must go. **Say:** "Critics of tech design have drawn the parallel with slot machines engineered for 'time on device' (Schüll 2012)."

**Schaffner et al. (2025), "An Experimental Study of Netflix Use and the Effects of Autoplay on Watching Behaviors," *Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact.* 9(2), CSCW.** doi:10.1145/3710928. In an experiment with 76 Netflix users, turning off autoplay cut viewing by **about 21 minutes a day** and shortened sessions by 17 minutes. ⚠️ Small and short. Still, it is the clearest causal evidence that removing a stopping cue increases consumption. **Say:** "In an experiment, switching off autoplay cut Netflix viewing by about 21 minutes a day (small study)." ⚠️ No randomised trial yet shows that *infinite scroll* increases time spent, so say it "removes natural stopping points," never "makes you scroll X% longer."

**Fitz et al. (2019), "Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being," *Computers in Human Behavior* 101: 84–94.** doi:10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. In a field experiment with 237 people, getting notifications in three daily batches made people feel "more attentive, productive, in a better mood, and in greater control of their phones." Removing notifications entirely *raised* anxiety and fear of missing out. **Why it matters:** soft, chosen limits beat total lockout, which matches ScrollJar's "never hard-locked" rule.

**Flayelle et al. (2023), "A taxonomy of technology design features that promote potentially addictive online behaviours," *Nature Reviews Psychology* 2(3): 136–50.** doi:10.1038/s44159-023-00153-4. An authoritative academic review of design features linked to loss of control, co-written by researchers who are critical of over-pathologising. Use the careful phrasing "potentially addictive."

**Sherman, Payton, Hernandez, Greenfield & Dapretto (2016), "The Power of the Like in Adolescence," *Psychological Science* 27(7): 1027–35.** doi:10.1177/0956797616645673. In a small fMRI study of **32 teenagers (13–18)**, seeing their own photos with many likes activated the nucleus accumbens, a reward region, and teens were more likely to "like" photos that already had many likes. ⚠️ Teenagers only, with no adult comparison, so "especially in adolescents" was wrong. Don't use it for adult claims.

## 3. Friction and limits work, with caveats

**Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023), "Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec," *PNAS* 120(8): e2213114120.** doi:10.1073/pnas.2213114120.
- **Field study:** 280 people who had installed one sec themselves, followed for 6 weeks. Users closed the app again on **36% of opening attempts**, attempts fell 37% from week 1 to week 6, and actual openings fell **57%**.
- **Online experiment:** preregistered, N = 500. The easy "dismiss" option had the strongest effect, a waiting delay also helped, and "the deliberation message was not effective."

⚠️ The field study had **no control group** and lost 61% of recruits. The 57% is *openings*, not time spent; the time saving was self-reported. A 2026 preprint of the same app found 65–70% of new users stop using it. **Say:** "In a six-week study of people who chose to use a pause app, users backed out of 36% of attempts to open an app, and openings fell by more than half. There was no control group, so it's promising rather than proof. In a separate randomised experiment, an easy way out worked best and a reflective message on its own did nothing."

**Mertens et al. (2026), Wellspent RCT, *JMIR mHealth and uHealth* 14: e56824.** doi:10.2196/56824. In a 3-week RCT with 70 adult iPhone users, a full-screen "close or continue" prompt after a self-set limit cut time on the most problematic app by about **29 minutes a day**. It did not reduce problematic-use scores. ⚠️ Small and short, but a peer-reviewed RCT of a mechanism very close to ScrollJar's.

**Castelo, Kushlev, Ward, Esterman & Reiner (2025), "Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being," *PNAS Nexus* 4(2): pgaf017.** doi:10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf017. A preregistered randomised trial with **467 adults**. Blocking all mobile internet for two weeks (calls and texts still worked) improved mental health, well-being and objectively measured sustained attention. Gains were linked to more time offline, more in-person connection, a greater sense of self-control and slightly more sleep. ⚠️ Only **25.5%** fully kept to the block. It blocked *all* mobile internet, a much stronger dose than blocking chosen apps. Don't quote "91% improved," which is a before-after count that chance alone would put near 87%. **Say:** "In a randomised trial, blocking mobile internet for two weeks improved attention, mental health and well-being, even though most people found the block hard to stick to."

**Monge Roffarello & De Russis (2023), meta-analysis of digital self-control tools, *ACM TOCHI* 30(4).** doi:10.1145/3571810. The pooled effect on time spent was g = 0.47, but from only 7 studies with weak designs. It is moderate support, not strong.

⚠️ **Counter-evidence to keep in mind:**
- Restricting social media to 10 minutes a day led students to switch to messaging apps, with no change in total device time or well-being (Collis & Eggers 2022, *PLOS ONE*).
- Effects often fade when tools are removed (Pieh 2025).
- Users snooze or switch off limits (Allcott 2022).

Design for retention and ongoing use, not a one-off effect.

## 4. Commitment devices and money stakes

**Giné, Karlan & Zinman (2010), "Put Your Money Where Your Butt Is: A Commitment Contract for Smoking Cessation," *AEJ: Applied Economics* 2(4): 213–35.** doi:10.1257/app.2.4.213. An RCT with 2,000 smokers in the Philippines. Smokers could deposit money that was **forfeited to charity** if a urine test showed smoking.
- Only **11%** took it up.
- Being offered it raised verified quitting by **3–6 percentage points** at 6 and 12 months, about **40%** above the comparison group's quit rate (the paper says 38–39%).
- 66% of those who signed up still lost their deposit.

**Why it matters:** this is the founding RCT for self-imposed forfeits, and the money went to charity, a direct precedent for ScrollJar. **Say:** "In a randomised trial, offering smokers a deposit they'd lose to charity if they smoked raised verified quitting by about 40%, even though few chose it."

**Halpern et al. (2015), "Randomized Trial of Four Financial-Incentive Programs for Smoking Cessation," *NEJM* 372(22): 2108–17.** doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1414293. The participants were 2,538 CVS employees, relatives and friends.
- Only 13.7% accepted deposit-based programs, against 90.0% for rewards, so overall the reward programs had higher quit rates (15.7% vs 10.2% at 6 months).
- Among people who would accept either, deposits were *more effective* by 13.2 percentage points (instrumental-variable analysis).
- ⚠️ The deposit arms also paid $650 in rewards, so they were not pure stakes.

**Why it matters:** for people who choose it, putting their own money on the line beats an equivalent reward, which is why stakes in ScrollJar must stay optional. Always keep the "among those willing" qualifier.

**Williams-Buttari et al. (2023), *Behavior Modification* 47(2): 476–503** (doi:10.1177/01454455221113561), and **Stinson & Dallery (2023), *JABA* 56(2): 323–35** (doi:10.1002/jaba.975). These are tiny single-case studies with 6 and 9 college students. Deposit or earn-back money contracts reduced phone and social-media use while they ran, with limited lasting effect. ⚠️ Very small. **Why it matters:** they are the only peer-reviewed tests of money stakes for phone use. **No published evaluation of any commercial stakes-based screen-time app (Forfeit, Opal or similar) exists yet.** That is ScrollJar's research opportunity, and it is the only accurate way to state the gap.

**de Buisonjé et al. (2022), "Investigating Rewards and Deposit Contract Financial Incentives for Physical Activity Behavior Change Using a Smartphone App: RCT," *J Med Internet Res* 24(10): e38339.** doi:10.2196/38339. An RCT with 126 people over 20 days. Financial incentives *as a group* increased exercise goal days compared with control. But:
- deposits had lower uptake (62% vs 100%) and were **not more effective than rewards** (P = .17);
- **loss-framed feedback did worse than gain-framed** (P = .007).

⚠️ It was previously cited here as support for deposits; it isn't. **Design lesson:** ScrollJar's feedback should be gain-framed (hours kept, money given), never loss-framed.

⚠️ **Counter-evidence to keep in mind:**
- People who choose commitment contracts don't always benefit. In a gym study, half who chose contracts for *more* attendance also chose contracts for *less* (Carrera et al. 2022, *Rev Econ Stud* 89(3)).
- In a commitment-contract platform study, 55% of users defaulted and paid (John 2020, *Management Science* 66(2)).
- Many ScrollJar users will simply pay their price, which is fine because it goes to charity. But we can't claim stakes "work" for everyone who sets one.

## 5. Penalty framing: fines, licensing, charity and kindness

**Gneezy & Rustichini (2000), "A Fine Is a Price," *Journal of Legal Studies* 29(1): 1–17.** doi:10.1086/468061. In a field study across 10 Israeli daycare centres, a small fine for late pickup *increased* lateness, and lateness stayed high after the fine was removed. The authors concede a large enough fee would reduce it. ⚠️ It is a single study, and a vignette replication found fines deterred (Metcalf et al. 2020). **Use as a design caution, not a law:** prices must be self-set and meaningful, and framed as a broken deal, not a purchase of scroll time.

**Gneezy, Imas & Madarász (2014), "Conscience Accounting: Emotion Dynamics and Social Behavior," *Management Science* 60(11): 2645–58.** doi:10.1287/mnsc.2014.1942. In lab experiments, people who had just lied were more likely to donate if asked straight away (73% vs 30% of truth-tellers), and the urge faded with delay. Two cautions:
- ⚠️ **Most liars, given the choice, chose to decide later.** Guilt was never measured.
- ⚠️ **Knowing a donation opportunity would follow increased lying from 48% to 63%** ("paying for sins").

That last finding is the most direct evidence that a known charity price can start to feel like a permit, which is exactly ScrollJar's risk. **Use it as a warning, not support.** Keep the broken-deal framing, the self-set meaningful price and the price-comfort watchdog, and measure repeat-override rates.

**Moral licensing.** Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg (2015), *PSPB* 41(4): 540–58 (doi:10.1177/0146167215572134), estimated d ≈ 0.31. But bias-corrected re-analyses put it between about zero and 0.18 (Kuper & Bott 2019, *Meta-Psychology* 3), and several replications failed. The largest meta-analysis, **Rotella et al. (2025), *PSPB*** (doi:10.1177/01461672251345512), covers 115 experiments and N = 21,770. Licensing appeared **when people were observed** (g = 0.51 after bias correction) and **not when unobserved** (g ≈ −0.01). **Why it matters:** ScrollJar's private-by-default override is the right design. ⚠️ This covers licensing from a good deed, not the "paying for sins" pathway above.

**Breines & Chen (2012), "Self-Compassion Increases Self-Improvement Motivation," *PSPB* 38(9): 1133–43.** doi:10.1177/0146167212445599. In four lab experiments with students, people prompted to be kind to themselves after a failure were more motivated to make amends and studied longer than those given a self-esteem boost. ⚠️ Small lab studies of motivation, with no direct replication. One-off self-compassion prompts did *not* change health behaviour in a meta-analysis (Phillips & Hine 2021, *Health Psychology Review* 15(1)). Only multi-session approaches did. **Say:** kindness after a slip supports retrying. Don't say it prevents relapse.

**Real-time studies of lapses** (Shiffman et al. 1996, *JCCP* 64(5): 993–1002; Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto 2012, *J Abnorm Psychol* 121(1): 187–97). These tracked smokers in real time: 203 smokers and 1,001 lapses in the later study. What predicted the slide from one slip to relapse was **feeling like giving up and losing confidence**; trying to get back on track straight away protected. Self-blame and guilt did *not* predict relapse. **Why it matters:** the after-slip screen should restore confidence and offer one small next step. This replaces the old claim that the "abstinence violation effect" was confirmed in these studies; it wasn't.

**Silverman & Barasch (2022/23), "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions," *J Consumer Research* 49(6): 1095–1117.** doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029. Seven studies. Showing a broken streak lowers later engagement. The drop is worse when people blame themselves, and softened when they can "repair" the streak. **Why it matters:** this is experimental support for kind, externalising copy ("a hard evening") and for offering a way back after a slip.

**Sharif & Shu (2021), "Nudging persistence after failure through emergency reserves," *OBHDP* 163: 17–29.** doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2019.01.004. Goals with a little built-in slack (an "emergency reserve") keep people going after a setback. **Why it matters:** this supports a planned allowance, such as a rough-day pass, so one slip doesn't end the deal.

**Implementation intentions.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38: 69–119, found d = 0.65 across 94 tests. The newer **Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024), *European Review of Social Psychology* 36(1): 162–94** (doi:10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563) covers 642 tests and finds effects of d = 0.27–0.66. They are larger when the plan has an if-then format, people are motivated, and the plan is rehearsed. **Say:** "If-then plans reliably help people act on their goals, especially when specific and rehearsed." Onboarding should ask for one, and let people revisit it.

## 6. Reduction beats abstinence, modestly

**Brailovskaia et al. (2023), "Finding the 'sweet spot' of smartphone use: Reduction or abstinence to increase well-being and healthy lifestyle?!," *J Exp Psych: Applied* 29(1): 149–61.** doi:10.1037/xap0000430. An experiment with 619 German adults:
- one group used no smartphone for 7 days;
- one cut daily use by 1 hour;
- a control group used their phones as usual.

Both interventions improved mood, life satisfaction and physical activity. "Most effects were stronger and remained more stable over 4 months in the reduction group." ⚠️ Self-reported, single lab group. Abstinence helped too, just less. **Say:** "Cutting phone use by an hour a day for a week improved mood and activity, with most gains lasting four months, more durably than a week of total abstinence."

**Lemahieu et al. (2025), *Scientific Reports* 15: 7581.** doi:10.1038/s41598-025-90984-3. A preregistered meta-analysis of social-media **abstinence** (10 studies, N = 4,674) found "no significant effects" on affect or life satisfaction. **Why it matters:** total breaks look weak, which supports ScrollJar's reduction model over detox.

**Radtke et al. (2022), "Digital detox: An effective solution in the smartphone era? A systematic literature review," *Mobile Media & Communication* 10(2): 190–215.** doi:10.1177/20501579211028647. Across 21 studies, effects were mixed: some positive, some null, some negative. (An earlier version of this file said effects "often fade"; that isn't in the review, so it's dropped.)

**Children and teens (not our audience):**
- **Schmidt-Persson et al. (2024), *JAMA Network Open* 7(7): e2419881.** Danish families handed in phones and tablets and cut leisure screen time to ≤3 hours a week for two weeks; children's emotional and social difficulties eased (d ≈ 0.5). Children, short, parent-reported.
- **Thai et al. (2024), *Psychology of Popular Media* 13(1): 162–69.** Distressed 17–25-year-olds limited to 1 hour a day for 3 weeks improved appearance and weight esteem slightly.

Use these only as context.

## 7. The adolescent-harm debate, stated fairly

**Orben & Przybylski (2019), *Nature Human Behaviour* 3(2): 173–82** (doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1). Across 355,358 adolescents, digital technology use explained **at most 0.4%** of well-being variation. In one dataset, regularly eating potatoes was associated with well-being nearly as negatively. Correlational. The other side and the consensus view:
- **Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024)** is a trade book arguing phones caused the post-2012 teen mental-health decline.
- **Odgers (2024), *Nature* 628: 29–30** is a book review calling that claim "not supported by science."
- **Haidt & Rausch (2026), *World Happiness Report 2026*, ch. 3** is an essay replying to Odgers, not a journal article.
- The **National Academies (2024)** found the literature "did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level."

**Honest synthesis for ScrollJar:** we don't need, and must not claim, a population-level catastrophe. Adult randomised trials show small but real benefits from cutting back, larger for some people, and from sustained reduction rather than detox. Our users are adults who *want* to change their own habit, and that individual case is solid.

## Removed in the October 2026 check
- **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)**, "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance," *Psychological Science*. **Retracted on 2 September 2026**: its data were judged untrustworthy, and a 2024–26 replication found no effect. Never cite it.
- **Shu et al. (2012)**, "Signing at the beginning makes ethics salient…," *PNAS*. **Retracted in 2021** (fabricated data). Relevant because of the "Sign your deal" feature: never cite it.
- Polarisation results from Allcott et al. (2020), which did not hold in 2020.
- The claim that tech insiders "explicitly borrowed" slot-machine patterns (not in Schüll).
- Any citation of **Brian Wansink**'s work on "stopping cues" or portion size (research misconduct, many retractions). Use the autoplay experiment above instead.
