# ScrollJar — Science verification, October 2026

**Checked:** 15 Oct 2026. About 70 citations from `science-dossier-bibliography.md`, `psychology-playbook.md`, `evidence-dossier.md` (Part 3), the website and the app prototype's "why this works" notes.

**Method:** six parallel checks, each covering one topic. Every claim was:
- read in the paper itself (abstract or full text via Crossref, PubMed, Europe PMC, NBER, PMC or author PDFs);
- checked for retractions, corrections and expressions of concern (Crossref update fields, OpenAlex);
- searched for failed replications and larger contradicting studies.

The detailed findings, with exact quotes and links, are in `science-verification-2026-10/` (files A–F).

---

## 1. Bottom line

The evidence behind ScrollJar holds up, but it is **more modest** than our docs claimed. The honest, defensible story:

- **Cutting back helps, modestly and for real.** Randomised trials in adults show small but consistent gains in well-being, mood, sleep and attention. They come from **sustained reduction**, not short detoxes, and total abstinence alone shows no reliable effect.
- **A pause with an easy way out works.** It measurably cuts app openings, and self-set limits cut time on social apps by about 16% in a large RCT.
- **Money stakes work for the minority who choose them**, including when forfeits go to charity (a smoking RCT did exactly that).
- **After a slip, confidence and a next step matter more than blame.**

Three things would have embarrassed us publicly. All are now fixed in the docs:
1. **A retracted paper.** Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) was **retracted on 2 Sep 2026**: its data were judged untrustworthy and a replication found no effect. The playbook said "replicated 2024." I confirmed the retraction in Crossref myself.
2. **A paper that cuts against us, cited as support.** "Conscience Accounting" (2014) shows that **knowing a donation will follow made people break rules *more* (48% → 63%).** That is ScrollJar's central risk, and the playbook cited the paper as proof that donating "repairs guilt."
3. **Brian Wansink cited in the app.** Two of the prototype's science notes cite him; he left Cornell after a research-misconduct finding and has many retracted papers.

## 3. Counter-evidence we must know

- **Effects are small.** The best adult estimates are 0.06–0.17 SD. Two meta-analyses (Ferguson 2024; Lemahieu 2025) find no average effect of abstinence. The National Academies (2024) say population-level harm to teens is "not supported." → Never imply large or clinical effects, or a teen "epidemic."
- **Short breaks can backfire.** Breaks under a week slightly worsened mental health in one reanalysis; a week or more helped (Thrul 2025). → Our message is sustained reduction, not detox.
- **People drop off and switch tools off.** 65–70% of new users stopped using one sec in a 2026 trial (preprint). Only 25.5% fully kept Castelo's block. People snooze limits (Allcott 2022), and use creeps back when support ends (Pieh 2025). → Retention is the real challenge, and it argues for an ongoing tool.
- **Commitment isn't for everyone.** Take-up of deposit contracts is 11–14% in big trials; many who sign up still pay (66% in Giné; 55% defaulted in John 2020). → Stakes stay optional; paying is fine (it goes to charity), but don't claim stakes "work" for everyone.
- **A known charity price can feel like a permit** (Conscience Accounting; "a fine is a price"). → Broken-deal framing, a meaningful self-set price, the price-comfort watchdog, and repeat-override tracking.
- **People judge commitment-device users.** They are trusted less than willpower users (Kristal & Zlatev 2024). → Private by default.
- **"Addiction" is contested** for phones and social media (Panova & Carbonell 2018; Billieux 2015). → Say "habit," "autopilot," "problematic use." This matches the Voice Law.

## 4. Strong new evidence added

| Study | Why it helps ScrollJar |
|---|---|
| **Castelo et al. 2025, *PNAS Nexus*** (RCT, 467 adults) | Blocking improved attention, mental health and well-being, and showed blocks are hard to keep: ScrollJar's niche |
| **Allcott et al. 2022, *AER*** (limits arm) | Self-set limits cut social-app time 16% over 12 weeks; 78% kept them |
| **Wellspent RCT 2026, *JMIR mHealth*** (70 adults) | A self-set iPhone "close or continue" prompt cut ~29 min/day on the most-used app; very close to our mechanism |
| **Pieh et al. 2025, *BMC Medicine*** (RCT, 111) | 2 h/day limit improved sleep, stress, mood; use rebounded after: supports an ongoing tool |
| **Burnell et al. 2025** (32 RCTs) | Small, reliable well-being gains from limiting social media |
| **Lindström et al. 2021, *Nature Communications*** | Human, real-world evidence that social media runs on reward learning |
| **Schaffner et al. 2025, CSCW** (76 people) | Switching off autoplay cut viewing ~21 min/day: removing stopping cues matters |
| **Fitz et al. 2019** (237 people) | Batched notifications → more control; total removal → more anxiety: supports "never hard-locked" |
| **Rotella et al. 2025, *PSPB*** (115 experiments) | Licensing appears only when watched: private overrides are right |
| **Silverman & Barasch 2022, *JCR*** | Kind, externalising copy and a way to repair after a slip |
| **Sharif & Shu 2021, *OBHDP*** | Built-in slack (a rough-day pass) keeps people going |
| **Sheeran et al. 2024** (642 tests) | If-then plans reliably help, especially when rehearsed |
| **Saeri et al. 2023; Cryder et al. 2013** | Concrete impact ("€5 ≈ 10 meals") makes giving meaningful |
| **Peetz et al. 2025; Harkin et al. 2016** | Sharing goals and tracking progress help: supports an optional "tell one person" feature and the weekly recap |

## 5. Proposed changes to the website and app (not made yet: awaiting L's OK)

These are public, so I haven't changed them. Each is a text-only edit, with no design change.

### Website (`landing/index.html`)
1. **"No screen-time app has been through a peer-reviewed trial yet — including ours."** This is **wrong**: the 36% study on our own cards *is* a peer-reviewed study of a screen-time app.
   → "We also read the studies that disagree with us: the benefits of cutting back are real, but modest. No app that puts money on the line has been through a published trial yet, including ours. We intend to be the first."
2. **Card "It isn't just you":** "Self-control problems drive roughly a third of social media use, and people will gladly pay for tools that help their future selves."
   → "A major study estimated that self-control problems drive about a third of time on social apps. Given the choice, most people set binding limits for themselves."
3. **Card "Friction alone changes behaviour":** the app combined a pause, a message and an easy way out, so "alone" overstates it.
   → Title: "A pause changes behaviour." Body: "With a brief pause before opening an app, people backed out of 36% of attempts. In a follow-up experiment, an easy way out did the most, and a lecture did nothing."
4. **Card "Blocking actually helps":** accurate. Optionally add: "…even though most people found the block hard to keep."
5. **How it works:** "a short pause before opening led people to drop 36% of app openings"
   → "…led people to back out of 36% of their attempts to open an app."

### App prototype "why this works" notes (`prototype/app.js`, and the same text in `scrolljar-prototype-v2.html`)
6. **Infinite scroll** and **Chosen vs drifting:** replace the Wansink sources with "Schaffner et al. 2025 (switching off autoplay cut viewing by ~21 min/day); Flayelle et al., *Nature Reviews Psychology* 2023."
7. **Variable reward:** remove the unverified Meta "think slot machines" quote and the word "dopamine" (our own rule bans it in user-facing copy).
   → "Rewards that arrive unpredictably are learned harder than reliable ones. That's basic reward learning, and people's behaviour on social media follows the same pattern. A feed that's occasionally great and usually ordinary keeps you checking." Source: Lindström et al., *Nature Communications* 2021; Schüll 2012.
8. **Social comparison:** "severe depression rose about 7% and anxiety about 20%, and the mechanism was unfavourable social comparison"
   → "When Facebook arrived on US campuses in 2004–06, students' mental health worsened, most of all for the most vulnerable, and unfavourable social comparison looked like a key reason."
9. **Bedtime procrastination:** "People with little autonomy during the day reclaim it at night by refusing to sleep — documented as bedtime procrastination."
   → "Researchers call it bedtime procrastination: going to bed later than you meant to, with nothing stopping you (Kroese et al. 2014). The popular idea that it's 'revenge' for a day without free time is plausible but barely studied."
10. **Checking habits:**
    - "Most phone opens are under 30 seconds" → "Many phone opens are quick checks".
    - "Roughly 43% of daily behaviour runs this way" → "In diary studies, about 43% of everyday actions happened almost daily in the same setting, often while thinking about something else".
    - "in the published trial of a similar tool" → "in a published study of a similar tool".
