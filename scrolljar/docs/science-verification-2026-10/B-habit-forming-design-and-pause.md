# B-findings: Addictive design (dossier §2), breath pause (playbook §2), "Never say" items 1–2

How I checked each one: Crossref metadata (looked at `update-to`, `updated-by` and `relation`), the PubMed abstract (efetch), the OpenAlex `is_retracted` flag, and full text where I could reach it (PMC, arXiv, Springer). None of the cited papers is retracted, and none has an expression of concern. Exceptions are noted inline.

## 1. Per-citation verification

### 1.1 Schultz, Dayan & Montague (1997)
- **Metadata (correct as we give it):** Schultz W, Dayan P, Montague PR. "A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward." *Science* 275(5306):1593–1599, 14 Mar 1997. doi:10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. PMID 9054347.
- **Opened:** Crossref, PubMed efetch, OpenAlex (9,347 citations, not retracted).
- **Quote:** "identifying dopaminergic neurons in the primate whose fluctuating output apparently signals changes or errors in the predictions of future salient and rewarding events."
- **Status:** A foundational and heavily replicated result in reward-prediction-error neuroscience. No corrections.
- **Verdict:** The paper's own finding (prediction error) is described **accurately**. The inference we draw from it ("the neuroscientific basis for why variable-reward feeds… are so compulsive") is **overstated**. That is a leap: recordings from single neurons in monkeys doing conditioning tasks become a claim about human app use and "compulsion." The paper says nothing about apps, humans or compulsion. Also, "unpredictable reward" is not the same thing as "variable-ratio schedule." The study that actually looks at reward *uncertainty* is Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz 2003 (verified below), and it too was done in monkeys.
- **Corrected wording:** "In monkeys, dopamine neurons respond most to rewards that are better than expected (a 'prediction error'), and that signal drives learning (Schultz et al. 1997). Many researchers see this as one reason why unpredictable rewards, such as a feed that sometimes shows something great, are so good at forming habits. Human studies of social media fit this reward-learning picture (Lindström et al. 2021), but nobody has directly shown that 'variable-reward feeds are compulsive' at the level of dopamine."
- **Optional add-on (verified):** Fiorillo CD, Tobler PN, Schultz W (2003). "Discrete Coding of Reward Probability and Uncertainty by Dopamine Neurons." *Science* 299(5614):1898–1902. doi:10.1126/science.1077349. Quote: "a previously unobserved response covaried with uncertainty and consisted of a gradual increase in activity until the potential time of reward", with uncertainty "maximal at P = 0.5". This is the closer fit if we want a variable-reward citation, but it is still monkeys.

### 1.2 Schüll (2012), *Addiction by Design*
- **Metadata (correct):** Natasha Dow Schüll, *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas*. Princeton University Press, 2012. ISBNs include 9780691127552 (hardcover), 9780691160887 (paperback) and 9781400834655 (ebook; Crossref doi:10.1515/9781400834655).
- **Opened:** Crossref book record, Open Library ISBN record, and a review in *Canadian Journal of Sociology* 40(4) 2015 by Cosgrave (doi:10.29173/cjs25708, PDF). The Princeton press page returned 403.
- **Quotes (from the review):** "designers and players learn from each other so that time-on-device is privileged over winning"; players "get lost in the 'zone.' What counts for these people is not wins, but rather, continuous play".
- **Verdict:** The ethnography of "time on device" and the "machine zone" is described **accurately**. The sentence "tech insiders explicitly borrowed these patterns" is **unverifiable from this source**. The book is about casino slot machines; it is not evidence that tech companies copied them. That claim needs its own source (for example, documented statements by named designers). Otherwise, drop it.
- **Corrected wording:** "Schüll's ethnography shows how slot machines are engineered to maximise 'time on device' and draw players into a trance-like 'machine zone.' Critics of tech design have since drawn the same parallel."

### 1.3 Sherman, Payton, Hernandez, Greenfield & Dapretto (2016)
- **Metadata (correct):** "The Power of the *Like* in Adolescence: Effects of Peer Influence on Neural and Behavioral Responses to Social Media." *Psychological Science* 27(7):1027–1035. doi:10.1177/0956797616645673. PMID 27247125, PMC5387999.
- **Opened:** PubMed abstract and the full text on PMC.
- **Sample (full text):** "Thirty-four typically developing adolescents (18 female; age range = 13–18 years) participated… Two of these 34 participants were excluded from fMRI data analysis". So **N = 32 analysed, adolescents only, with no adult comparison group.** The authors say the sample size "reflects the maximum number of participants that we were able to recruit given available funding."
- **Key quotes:** "there was greater activity in the left NAcc when participants viewed neutral images that had many likes… We also observed greater bilateral NAcc activation when participants viewed their own images for the many likes > few likes contrast. For images depicting risk-taking behavior, likes had no effect on brain response in the NAcc". On behaviour: "They were more likely to like photos depicted with many likes than photos with few likes."
- **Status:** Not retracted and no corrections. Single small fMRI study; I found no direct replication.
- **Verdict:** **Wrong on "especially in adolescents."** The study tested only adolescents, so it cannot show the effect is stronger in adolescents than in anyone else. "Peers' likes shift behavior" is **overstated**: the only behaviour measured was clicking "like" in a lab simulation of Instagram. Also, ScrollJar is for adults and this is adolescent-only evidence.
- **Corrected wording:** "In a small fMRI study of 32 teenagers (13–18), seeing their own photos with many likes activated the nucleus accumbens, a key reward region. The teens were also more likely to 'like' photos that already had many likes (Sherman et al. 2016)." If we want evidence about adults, use Lindström et al. 2021 instead.

### 1.4 Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita (2012)
- **Metadata:** "Habits make smartphone use more pervasive." *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 16(1):105–114. doi:10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2. Published online 16 Jun 2011; issue dated 2012. Citing it as 2012 is fine, but add the issue number (1).
- **Opened:** Crossref, and the Springer abstract page (plain curl).
- **Quotes:** "The form of habits we identified is called a checking habit: brief, repetitive inspection of dynamic content quickly accessible on the device… checking habits occasionally spur users to do other things with the device and may increase usage overall. Data from a controlled field experiment show that checking behaviors emerge and are reinforced by informational 'rewards' that are very quickly accessible. Qualitative data suggest that although repetitive habitual use is frequent, **it is experienced more as an annoyance than an addiction**."
- **Status:** Not retracted. Small field studies from 2009–2010, in the early smartphone era.
- **Verdict:** The description of the paper is **mostly accurate**, but "smartphone use is *dominated* by checking habits" goes further than the source. The paper says checking is frequent and "may increase usage overall." Our "Why it matters" line ("scrolling is habit, not decision") is **overstated**: the paper studies brief *checking*, not scrolling. Note also that the paper says users experienced the habit as an annoyance rather than an addiction, which cuts against our "slot machine" framing (although it fits ScrollJar's non-pathologising voice).
- **Corrected wording:** "Much smartphone use comes as brief, automatic 'checks' triggered by context and reinforced by quick bits of new information. Users tend to describe them as an annoying habit rather than an addiction (Oulasvirta et al. 2012). That's why ScrollJar targets the moment of reaching for the phone instead of relying on willpower."

### 1.5 Bowen & Marlatt (2009), urge surfing
- **Metadata (we give no DOI; here is the correct one):** Bowen S, Marlatt A. "Surfing the urge: brief mindfulness-based intervention for college student smokers." *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors* 23(4):666–671. doi:**10.1037/a0017127**. PMID 20025372. Note that 10.1037/a0017811 is a different paper; do not use it.
- **Quote:** "Undergraduate smokers (N = 123)… groups did not differ significantly on measures of urges. However, those in the mindfulness group smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over a 7-day follow-up period… The study provides preliminary data".
- **Status:** Not retracted. A single small RCT in students with a 7-day follow-up; the authors themselves call it preliminary.
- **Verdict:** **Accurate** ("reduced smoking without reducing urge frequency"). Strictly, the paper measured urge *intensity/measures*, not frequency. Flag it as preliminary.
- **Suggested wording:** "In a small trial with student smokers, a brief 'urge surfing' instruction didn't make cravings weaker, but people smoked fewer cigarettes over the next week. It changed how they responded to the urge, not the urge itself (preliminary evidence)."

### 1.6 Westbrook et al. (2013)
- **Metadata:** Westbrook C, Creswell JD, Tabibnia G, Julson E, Kober H, Tindle HA. "Mindful attention reduces neural and self-reported cue-induced craving in smokers." *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 8(1):73–84. doi:10.1093/scan/nsr076 (online 2011; issue 2013). PMID 22114078.
- **Quote:** "Forty-seven (n = 47) meditation-naïve treatment-seeking smokers… mindful attention reduced self-reported craving to smoking images, and reduced neural activity in… subgenual anterior cingulate cortex".
- **Status:** Not retracted. Small, lab-based, single study.
- **Verdict:** **Accurate.** It is about smokers' craving in response to cues; the extension to phone urges is an analogy.
- **Note on internal consistency:** Bowen & Marlatt found no change in urges, while Westbrook found reduced craving. Our sentence "The pause isn't there to kill the urge" leans on Bowen & Marlatt, which is fine, but say "may soften the urge, and helps you not act on it."

### 1.7 Wilson et al. (2014), "Just think"
- **Metadata (correct):** Wilson TD, Reinhard DA, Westgate EC, Gilbert DT, Ellerbeck N, Hahn C, Brown CL, Shaked A. "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind." *Science* 345(6192):75–77. doi:10.1126/science.1250830. PMID 24994650, PMC4330241.
- **Opened:** PubMed abstract and PMC full text.
- **Exact result:** "67% of men (12 of 18) gave themselves at least one shock during the thinking period… compared to 25% of women (6 of 24)… Note that these results only include participants who had reported that they would pay to avoid being shocked again." The thinking period was 15 minutes; participants were college students; one outlier gave himself 190 shocks.
- **Replication:** The core finding that people enjoy "doing" more than "thinking for pleasure" replicated across cultures. Buttrick, Choi, Wilson et al. (2019) *JPSP* 117(5):e71–e83, doi:10.1037/pspp0000198: "2,557 participants from 12 sites in 11 countries. The results were consistent in every country." Interestingly, reported phone use was *negatively* correlated with enjoying thinking. The shock behaviour itself is supported by a small study: Havermans et al. (2015) *Appetite* 85:52–57, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2014.11.007 (N=30; people "more readily self-administered electrical shocks when bored"). The shock numbers come from small subsamples (42 people).
- **Verdict:** "People prefer electric shocks to sitting with their thoughts" is **overstated**. Not "people" in general: 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women gave themselves at least one shock in 15 minutes. Everyone else did not.
- **Corrected wording:** "Many people find even 15 minutes alone with their thoughts surprisingly hard. In one study, two-thirds of men and a quarter of women chose to give themselves a mild electric shock rather than just sit and think (Wilson et al., Science 2014). The preference for 'doing' over 'thinking' has held up in 11 countries."

### 1.8 Elhai et al. (2018)
- **Metadata:** Elhai JD, Vasquez JK, Lustgarten SD, Levine JC, Hall BJ. "Proneness to Boredom Mediates Relationships Between Problematic Smartphone Use With Depression and Anxiety Severity." *Social Science Computer Review* 36(6):707–720. doi:10.1177/0894439317741087 (online Nov 2017; issue 2018).
- **Opened:** Crossref and the OpenAlex abstract (not indexed in PubMed).
- **Quote:** "Using a cross-sectional design, we surveyed 298 American college students… boredom proneness predicted PSU, but not SUF [smartphone use frequency]."
- **Status:** Not retracted. **Cross-sectional and self-reported**; "predicted" here is a statistical term from structural equation modelling, not a causal one.
- **Verdict:** **Accurate as worded** ("predicts" is the paper's word), but readers will hear causation. Note also that boredom did *not* predict how often people used their phones. A stronger replacement now exists: Tagliaferri et al. (2026) meta-analysis, in §3.
- **Corrected wording:** "People who get bored easily are more likely to report problematic phone use (r ≈ .38 across 25 studies; correlational)."

### 1.9 "Never say" item 1: dopamine detox
- **Source:** Grinspoon P. "Dopamine fasting: Misunderstanding science spawns a maladaptive fad." Harvard Health Blog, 26 Feb 2020. https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/dopamine-fasting-misunderstanding-science-spawns-a-maladaptive-fad-2020022618917 (opened). Note the title: "spawns a maladaptive fad", not "spurs a myth."
- **Quotes:** "while dopamine does rise in response to rewards or pleasurable activities, it doesn't actually decrease when you avoid overstimulating activities, so a dopamine 'fast' doesn't actually lower your dopamine levels"; people wrongly believe "depleted dopamine stores will have replenished themselves. Sadly, it doesn't work that way at all." The creator, Sepah, is quoted saying "The title's not to be taken literally."
- **Verdict:** Calling it "pseudoscience" is fair in substance, but Harvard's words are "faulty science / misinterpretation." "Dopamine doesn't deplete or reset" is slightly **overstated**: Harvard says *avoiding stimulation doesn't lower dopamine*, and the blog itself uses the word "reset" in a non-literal CBT sense. This is a physician's blog post, not peer-reviewed. Keep the rule.
- **Corrected wording:** "'Dopamine detox' misreads the science. Avoiding stimulating activities doesn't lower or 'refill' your dopamine (Harvard Health, 2020). The useful part, breaking cue-driven habits, is ordinary behaviour change."

### 1.10 "Never say" item 2: ego depletion
- **Hagger et al. (2016):** "A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect." *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4):546–573. doi:10.1177/1745691616652873. Quote: "k = 23, total N = 2,141… d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]". **Verified.**
- **Vohs et al. (2021):** "A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect." *Psychological Science* 32(10):1566–1581. doi:10.1177/0956797621989733. Quote: "k = 36; N = 3,531… Confirmatory tests found a nonsignificant result (d = 0.06)… data were 4 times more likely under the null… Exploratory analyses on the full sample… statistically significant effect (d = 0.08)". **Verified.** It was led by an original proponent, which makes it especially credible.
- **Verdict:** "failed multi-lab replications (d ≈ 0.04)" is **accurate**. Add Vohs 2021 for robustness. To be fully honest: the effect is not proven to be exactly zero; at most it is tiny.
- **Suggested wording (internal):** "Two large preregistered multi-lab tests (≈2,100 and ≈3,500 people) found effects close to zero (d = 0.04 and 0.06)."

## 2. Counter-evidence (verified)

1. **Whether "smartphone/social media addiction" is a real addiction is contested.** Panova & Carbonell (2018), "Is smartphone addiction really an addiction?" *J Behav Addict* 7(2):252–259, doi:10.1556/2006.7.2018.49: "we did not find sufficient support from the addiction perspective to confirm the existence of smartphone addiction… could be better labeled as problematic or maladaptive smartphone use". Billieux et al. (2015), "Are we overpathologizing everyday life?" *J Behav Addict* 4(3):119–123, doi:10.1556/2006.4.2015.009, warns that "studies overpathologizing daily life activities are likely to prompt a dismissive appraisal". **What it means for us:** a section headed "Addictive design: the slot machine in your pocket" invites exactly this critique. Say "habit-forming / attention-capturing design" and "problematic use." Do not call people's use "addiction." This fits the Voice Law, and it fits Oulasvirta's own "annoyance rather than addiction" finding.
2. **Self-report and "problematic use" scales track actual use poorly.** Parry et al. (2021), *Nature Human Behaviour* 5(11):1535–1547, doi:10.1038/s41562-021-01117-5. This is a preregistered meta-analysis of 106 effect sizes: "self-reported media use correlates only moderately with logged measurements… measures of problematic media use show an even weaker association with usage logs." **What it means for us:** correlational findings built on these scales (Elhai, the boredom meta-analysis) are weak evidence about actual behaviour. ScrollJar's logged openings data are more credible; lean on behavioural RCTs.
3. **Infinite scroll: there is no rigorous causal evidence on time spent.** I searched and found no randomised trial comparing an infinite feed with a paginated one on time spent. The nearest studies: Ruiz, Molina León & Heuer (2024), *Mensch und Computer 2024*, doi:10.1145/3670653.3677495. With N=30, a friction interface (reacting to each post to load more) "exhibited significantly better content recall, although a majority of participants found the interface frustrating"; this measured memory, not time. Meinhardt et al. (2025), CHI '25, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713187 (N=72, 7 days), tested *interventions during* infinite scrolling. Its opening sentence "designed to encourage prolonged engagement" is the authors' framing, not something they measured. **What it means for us:** never claim "studies show infinite scroll makes you scroll X% longer." Say it "removes natural stopping cues."
4. **Dopamine hype:** see 1.1 and 1.9. Even the European Parliament resolution (below) repeats "intermittent variable reward, leading to a dopamine surge, just like… slot machines." That is a political text, not evidence. Do not cite it as evidence of a dopamine mechanism.
5. **Removing notifications entirely can backfire.** Fitz et al. (2019), in §3, found that the no-notifications group "experienced higher levels of anxiety and 'fear of missing out' (FoMO)." **What it means for us:** this supports soft friction and choice over total lockout, which is consistent with Grüning 2023's easy-dismiss finding.

## 3. New studies to add (verified)

1. **Lindström et al. (2021), *Nature Communications* 12:1311.** doi:10.1038/s41467-020-19607-x. A publisher correction (doi:10.1038/s41467-021-22067-6) fixed only the data-availability statement and reporting summary, not the results. Quote: "We analyze over one million posts from over 4000 individuals… human behavior on social media conforms qualitatively and quantitatively to the principles of reward learning… an online experiment (n = 176)… verifies that social rewards causally influence behavior". *Why it matters:* it is the missing bridge from monkey dopamine to real adult social-media behaviour, and it includes a causal experiment. *Claim:* "Large-scale analyses of real social-media posting show people respond to likes the way reward-learning theory predicts."
2. **Schaffner et al. (2025), "An Experimental Study Of Netflix Use and the Effects of Autoplay on Watching Behaviors," *Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact.* 9(2) (CSCW 2025).** doi:10.1145/3710928 (arXiv 2412.16040). Quote: "conducted an experimental study with 76 Netflix users… disabling Netflix's autoplay resulted in, on average, 21 fewer minutes watched per day and 17-minute shorter sessions." *Why it matters:* it is the clearest causal evidence I could find that a specific attention-capture feature increases consumption in adults. It is small (N=76), US-only and short. *Claim:* "In a randomised study, switching off autoplay cut Netflix viewing by about 21 minutes a day (small study)."
3. **Fitz, Kushlev, Jagannathan, Lewis, Paliwal & Ariely (2019), "Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being," *Computers in Human Behavior* 101:84–94.** doi:10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016 (abstract opened via the Semantic Scholar API; Elsevier blocked). Quote: "randomized field experiment (n = 237)… participants whose notifications were batched three-times-a-day felt more attentive, productive, in a better mood, and in greater control of their phones… participants who did not receive notifications at all… experienced higher levels of anxiety and… FoMO". The outcomes are self-reported. Note that the abstract contradicts itself: it says "more… productive" and then "lower productivity." Cite only the "more attentive, productive, in greater control" part. Supporting study: Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn (2016), CHI '16:1011–1020, doi:10.1145/2858036.2858359: with N=221 in a within-person design, "higher levels of inattention and hyperactivity when alerts were on than when alerts were off." *Claim:* "Getting notifications in a few predictable batches, rather than constantly, helped people feel more in control of their phones."
4. **Tagliaferri et al. (2026), "Trait Boredom and Problematic Use of New Technologies: A Meta-Analysis," *Psychological Reports*.** doi:10.1177/00332941261459995 (Europe PMC abstract; volume and pages not yet assigned). Quote: "25 studies (n = 15,152)… moderate positive association between trait boredom and problematic digital technology use (r = .38, 95% CI [.32, .43]), with no evidence of publication bias." *Why it matters:* it replaces the single-study Elhai citation; it is still correlational. *Claim:* "Across 25 studies, people who are more prone to boredom report more problematic tech use (correlational)."
5. **Flayelle, Brevers, King, Maurage, Perales & Billieux (2023), "A taxonomy of technology design features that promote potentially addictive online behaviours," *Nature Reviews Psychology* 2(3):136–150.** doi:10.1038/s44159-023-00153-4 (abstract opened on nature.com). Quote: "we critically examine the available research on the relationships between technology design features and the loss of control… [and] propose a theory-driven general taxonomy". *Why it matters:* it is an authoritative academic synthesis, co-written by the "overpathologizing" critic Billieux, which makes it hard to dismiss. The careful phrasing is "*potentially* addictive."
- **Policy context (not scientific evidence):** European Parliament resolution of 12 Dec 2023, "Addictive design of online services and consumer protection in the EU single market" (TA-9-2023-0459; full text opened via the EP open-data XML). Quotes: it lists "'infinite scroll', 'pull-to-refresh' page reload, 'never ending auto-play'… 'recapture notifications'" and asks for a digital "'right not to be disturbed'… turning all attention-seeking features off by design." It is non-binding. Use it as "lawmakers recognise the problem," never as scientific proof.
- **Optional:** Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015), *JEP: Human Perception and Performance* 41(4):893–897, doi:10.1037/xhp0000100: "cellular phone notifications alone significantly disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task." It is a single lab study.

## 4. Top 3 risks

1. **Sherman 2016 "especially in adolescents."** This is factually wrong: the sample was 32 teenagers and there was no adult group. A scientist will spot it immediately. **Fix:** "in a small fMRI study of 32 teenagers…", and for adults lean on Lindström 2021.
2. **The leap from monkey dopamine to "slot machine in your pocket" / "variable-reward feeds are compulsive."** Schultz 1997 is about prediction errors in monkey neurons. "Tech insiders borrowed these patterns" is not in Schüll. The addiction framing is contested (Panova & Carbonell; Billieux), and Oulasvirta's own users called checking an "annoyance, not an addiction." It is also awkward that we ban "dopamine" from marketing while the dossier leans on it. **Fix:** retitle the section along the lines of "Habit-forming design: why the reach is automatic." Present dopamine as a mechanism researchers think contributes, and cite the causal feature-level evidence (autoplay RCT, notification batching) instead of neuron recordings.
3. **Wilson 2014 "people prefer electric shocks to sitting with their thoughts."** It is a quotable line that falls apart under scrutiny: 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women, in a 15-minute session. **Fix:** give the exact numbers ("two-thirds of men, a quarter of women, in a 15-minute session") and cite the 11-country replication of the doing-over-thinking preference. Also mark Elhai and the boredom link as correlational, and Bowen & Marlatt as preliminary (N=123, 7 days).

Smaller fixes: correct the Bowen & Marlatt DOI to 10.1037/a0017127. The Harvard blog title is "…spawns a maladaptive fad." The Oulasvirta paper is about *checking*, not scrolling. Add Vohs 2021 (d=0.06, N=3,531) next to Hagger 2016.
